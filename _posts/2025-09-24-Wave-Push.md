*Why swell data alone won’t tell you what board to bring*

---

## The Myth of the Magic Metric

One of the most common pieces of feedback I get on my forecasts was:  
> “You should include the period—it tells you how powerful the wave is.”  

And yeah, I get it. Period *sounds* like it should be a reliable indicator of push. Or maybe kilojoules. Or wave height. Or some magic number that tells you exactly how hard that wave is going to hit. But here’s the truth: **there’s no single reliable metric for push**.

Push is a cocktail of swell energy and local bathymetry. And unless you’re surfing a reef break that behaves like clockwork, you’re not going to get a clean answer from the data alone.

---

## The One-Board Problem

Back when I lived close enough to bike to the beach, I had room for one board on the rack. That meant I had to guess what the waves were doing, and believe it or not, I used to guess wrong a lot. But going home to swap boards? That’s defeat, and *I would never*.

I even toyed with building an app that would just tell you what board to bring based on the forecast. I spent a ton of time trying to build algorithms that could do it. Spoiler: **they didn’t work**.

---

## Why Energy Still Matters (Sort Of)

I still think wave energy is worth looking at. It combines wave height and period, and that’s useful. But on the East Coast, things get weird. We get a mix of:

- **Windswell** (short period, local storms, common)
- **Mid-period swell** (common in winter)
- **Long-period swell** (hurricanes or the rare, huge winter swell)

Unlike the West Coast, where long-period swell is frequent and reliable, our long-period swell is often small or only part of the overall picture. You’ll see something like:

- `0.7' @ 13s` → looks promising on a period chart, but invisible on swell height.  
- `5' @ 5s` → looks big, but it’s just junky windswell with strong onshores

So if you’re only looking at height or only looking at period, you're only seeing half the picture.

---

## Real-World Examples of Push (or Lack Thereof)

Let’s talk about some actual surf days:

- **Waist-high with perfect shape**: Think Trestles in California. Even thigh-high can be shortboardable if it’s breaking clean.  
- **New Jersey shorebreak**: I’ve had days where `3.5' @ 7s` was steep, fast, and fun. But most of the time, that same swell is longboard territory.  
- **Chest-high with no wall**: This summer I surfed chest-high swell at ~9s, but the direction was off and the sand was bad. Flat spots right after takeoff. Couldn’t even keep a groveler going half the time.

So yeah, sometimes it’s easier to shortboard a knee-high wave than a chest-high one, and period or energy doesn't capture that.

---

## Why Forecasting Is Hard (Especially Here)

If you’re surfing J Bay or Pipeline, you can pretty much trust the data. Those breaks are consistent, and the bathymetry doesn’t change much. But on the East Coast (or any sand-bottom beach break) everything shifts:

- Sandbars move  
- Storms reshape the bottom  
- A spot that worked last year might be dead this year  
- Swell that’s thigh-high and mushy one spot might be two feet bigger just a mile away

---

## What I Actually Care About

So here’s my philosophy:

- **Is there enough swell to make it worth showing up?**  
- **Is the wind light or offshore enough to keep things clean?**  
- **Is it worth driving around to find a working bar?**

I do have my own energy calculations that I've worked at for a while that I believe are the best indicator of wave power that I've found to date. But at the end of the day, it's only part of the equation.
